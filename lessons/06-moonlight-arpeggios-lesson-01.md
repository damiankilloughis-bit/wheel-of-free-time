# Wish #6: Moonlight Sonata, 3rd mvt. Opening arpeggios
## Lesson 1: Even first, then speed (15 minutes)

### Where you are (your numbers)
| | You | Recording | Your % |
|---|---|---|---|
| First arpeggio (start → top chords) | 4.26 s | 2.00 s | 47% |
| Start of next arpeggio | 4.80 s | 2.12 s | 44% |
| **Gap: top chords → restart** (4.80 − 4.26) | **0.54 s** | **0.12 s** | **22%** |

**The main finding:** the arpeggio itself is at about 47% speed, but the jump back down to start the next one is at about 22%. The jump is your biggest relative loss, so it gets its own drill today.

**Metronome conversion:** I'm assuming one arpeggio plus its two chords = 2 bars = 8 quarter-note clicks. On that basis your current speed is about **♩ = 100** (8 × 60 / 4.80) and the recording is about **♩ = 226**. *Check this in minute 0. If the cycle doesn't come out at 8 clicks, write down how many clicks it took and I'll redo the numbers.*

**Today's rule (Gebrian, Boyd):** practice at a speed so slow you can't make mistakes. When it starts falling apart, slow down, play it once at a solid speed, and stop for the day. Wrong notes at the plateau mean your brain is getting more than it can process at that speed. They don't mean you lack talent.

---

### Before you start (fixing "I can't hear the click")
Pick **one**:
- **Click in your ears:** run a phone metronome app or a metronome with a headphone jack into earbuds. Kate Boyd uses a quartz metronome with a headphone jack and volume control for this.
- **Feel it:** use a vibrating metronome (e.g., a Soundbrenner-style watch). Jazer Lee: "vibrations are really hard to ignore". *(His video has a sponsor code, so he's not neutral.)*
- **Digital piano?** Turn the piano's volume down. Either way, play **soft** today. Every source says a fast arpeggio is light, close to the keys, and with a quiet wrist.
- **Double-time click:** if one click per beat is hard to follow, set the click twice as fast (♩ = 100 → 200 eighth-note clicks) and play at the same speed. Jazer Lee says the extra click in between "helps you stay in time".

### The session (minute by minute)

**0:00–1:00 Calibrate**
- Metronome at ♩ = 100. Play one arpeggio plus chords plus the start of the next. Does the cycle take 8 clicks? Note it.

**1:00–3:00 Rhythm away from the keys (hands on your lap, metronome on)**
- 1:00–2:00: click at **80**. Tap 4 even taps per click on your thigh with your right fingers 1-2-3-5, counting aloud "1-e-and-a". Land *exactly* on the click, not before or after (Ashlee Young; Emma Blair).
- 2:00–3:00: same at **100**. You're building an internal pulse, so you stop chasing the click. As Ashlee Young puts it: "If you're waiting to hear the click you'll always be a little bit late."

**3:00–5:00 Block the hand positions (right hand alone, no metronome, soft)**
- Play the arpeggio as **blocked shapes** instead of single notes, moving through the positions up the keyboard. Then play it as single notes "but still moving as if you're playing chords" (Jodie Kim, 0:30–3:40).
- Keep the hand **inside** on the black keys: don't pull out to the white keys and back in, because that twist costs time (Jodie Kim ~8:30; Edna Golandsky ~4:52).
- Let the thumb **tuck inward toward its next note** instead of staying stretched out (Brillante Piano Duo 1:10).

**5:00–8:00 Long–short exercise (right hand alone, no metronome)** *(fixes choppy, uneven playing)*
Danae Dörken's exercise (demo: 2:56). One long note, then the next three as fast as you can, then stop on the next long note. Rotate which note is long:
- 5:00–5:45: long on note **1** of each group of four
- 5:45–6:30: long on note **2**
- 6:30–7:15: long on note **3**
- 7:15–8:00: long on note **4**
- While holding the long note, **stay still and relaxed.** Get the next finger ready only "in a split second" right before it plays, because that's all the time you'll have at full speed.

**8:00–11:00 The jump-back drill** *(your 22% problem)*
Practice only the **last 2 notes of the arpeggio, the 2 top chords, and the first 4 notes of the next arpeggio**.
- 8:00–9:00: no metronome. Use the top chords as a **release**. Danae "flicks the wrist forward" there before starting the next arpeggio (7:13). Edna Golandsky: the chord "sends" the arm, upper arm, and torso down with a rotation; jumping without it is "much bigger movement… and slower" (8:00). Then do just the **chord → first note of the next arpeggio**, landing accurately (PianoTechSupport's jump practice, 7:53).
- 9:00–11:00: metronome **♩ = 100**. The chords land on their clicks, and the next arpeggio starts *on time*, with no extra pause. Once that's clean three times in a row, add one note before and one after, then another before and after (Danae's isolation method, 8:13).

**11:00–14:00 Short bursts with rests (click up by small steps)**
Molly Gebrian's chunking with rests (Cora Cooper method, 18:04): play **one beat + one note** (5 notes) as a burst, then **rest one beat**, then the next beat + note, and so on through the arpeggio.
- 11:00–12:00: ♩ = **100**
- 12:00–13:00: ♩ = **104**, then **108**
- 13:00–14:00: ♩ = **112**, then **116** *only if 108 was clean*
- Brillante Piano Duo: go up only "about four numbers at a time", and only when it's accurate and relaxed. Gebrian: the rests are for relaxing your hand and clearing your mind.

**14:00–15:00 One clean run and stop**
- Whole arpeggio plus jump plus next start at **♩ = 92**, once, soft and even. Stop there; don't push for one more fast run (Kate Boyd).
- **Write down:** fastest clean burst tempo, fastest clean whole-cycle tempo, and (optional) a stopwatch time for the cycle.

### What people who struggled with this said
- **Ben Laude** (professional pianist, multiple degrees, being coached by Taubman teacher Robert Durso, tonebase): "Broken anything has been the bane of my existence for 20, 30 years." His uneven middle notes evened out once he stopped stopping on the thumb and moved his arm in one small "over" shape to the next thumb. Durso: "When you stop on the thumb, that's actually the first note of a group… you're making this the end." Afterwards: "you do the correct things and it wants to go fast." https://youtu.be/n2oIDlFVEzo?t=126 (2:06; result at ~8:32)
- **Easy Riders** (amateur who once quit as a kid, self-taught, 3 months at ~2 h/day): he'd tried the piece 2 years earlier "and gave up because it was way too difficult." Even after 3 months, "there's still loads of mistakes… playing loads of things out of tempo." His advice is to put a version out anyway rather than wait until it's perfect. https://youtu.be/hVHf5Fo7BuQ?t=81
- **WattKeys** (21-year-old performer, 81-min breakdown): he practiced the runs slowly with a "plucking staccato." At speed you can't pluck every note, so it "smooths out" into a crisp touch. He admits his left hand "becomes a rattling mess" above about 140–145, so he chose a slower tempo than Valentina Lisitsa's (~185). https://youtu.be/7ZxcN-gh020?t=494 (8:14; 20:16)
- **Molly Gebrian** (violist, teaches brain-based practice): she couldn't play a passage for a premiere, "practiced it every way I could think of," then invented interleaved clicking up "out of frustration… and it was like magic." (Planned for lesson 2.) https://youtu.be/75OWZAq-O4U?t=92 (1:32)
- **Jazer Lee's student "Sean":** clapping, singing, and the metronome all failed. Lightly tapping the beat on his shoulder while he played worked, and after two months he could keep the beat himself. https://youtu.be/ZfNG_LFN2F8?t=30

*A disagreement worth knowing about:* Danae Dörken, Ugnius, and Molly Gebrian swear by rhythm variations (long–short, dotted). The Taubman teacher (Durso) argues that stopping on the thumb trains the wrong ending. Today's long–short exercise follows Danae's version: stay relaxed while holding, and get the next finger ready only at the last moment. Lesson 3 tests the other view.
